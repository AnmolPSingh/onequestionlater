## Pick one base before connecting anything

People often have email in three places and files in two more. Some of those services don't let AI tools connect at all. The first step is choosing one base for email and one for files, forwarding everything else into it, and only then connecting the tools. Skipping this is why setups break a week later.

## A senior manager and a secretary

A pattern that works for busy leaders: one AI for deep work (strategy papers, drafts, thinking through a decision) and a lighter one for logistics (moving files, quick messages, bookings). Asking one tool to do both usually means it does neither well.

## One folder per workstream

When everything lives in one place, the AI's memory blurs: board work leaks into the book project, the book project into the restaurant. Separate workstations, each with its own context, keep answers sharp. It's the same reason you don't keep every client's papers in one drawer.

## Drafts, not sends

When AI writes emails for someone, I set it to draft only. The person stays the gatekeeper and reads before anything leaves. Trust gets built in drafts, and it's easy to loosen later.

## Start with one live problem

Setup sessions go best when we bring a real task to the table, like a rota for thirty staff across three sites or prep for a panel next week. Building around something that matters this week teaches more than any tour of features.

## Talk instead of type

Most people think faster than they type. Voice-to-text across every app, with a dictionary that learns your names and places, is often the single change that gives someone the most time back.

## Keep your knowledge yours

A second brain should live somewhere you control, in plain files, so it moves with you if you ever change tools. The value is in what you've collected, not in which model reads it.
