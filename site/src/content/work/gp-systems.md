## The question

Most of what I've built at Global Philanthropic runs on private data and inside private relationships, so that's where it stays. What I can show you is how I think about it, because that part travels.

The first question I ask isn't "what can we automate?" It's "why does this exist, and what is it connected to?"

## What I found

Make the oven faster and the queue just moves to the person plating. A lot of automation works like that. One task gets quicker, and the pressure lands on the next person along.

A charity isn't a list of tasks. It's a system: donors, data, boards, staff, trust, and the long delay between asking for something and finding out if it worked.

The writer and systems scientist Donella Meadows ranked the places you can step into a system in [Leverage Points](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/). Numbers sit near the bottom. They get most of the attention and rarely change how a system behaves. Further up come who can see what information, then the rules, then the goals, then the mindset the whole thing grows from.

That's how I read this sector. If a report takes a week, the obvious fix is a faster report. The better question is who needs to see what, and when, and whether they'd decide anything differently if they could.

John Kania, Mark Kramer and Peter Senge make a similar case for funders in [The Water of Systems Change](https://www.fsg.org/resource/water_of_systems_change/): the conditions holding a problem in place are often the least visible ones, and funders have to look at their own part in them.

## What I tried

- I map how the work actually happens, not how the process document says it does.
- I look for the gaps, the patterns and the loops, and I start by taking things out.
- Not everything needs AI. Some of what I've deployed uses it and some doesn't. The test is the same either way: does this give someone their time back?
- I train the people around me to own these systems, so a one-off fix turns into how the organisation works.

## What it became

Manual workload dropped by about 35%, measured against how long the same tasks took before automation. The bigger change is that it stuck.

## The next question

What would change if every organisation asked why something exists before asking how to speed it up?
